## Contributions

### Agent: Strategist

#### Position
Option C (Hybrid) is the strategically correct move, but only if the 60-day B2B test is treated as a real commitment with measurable kill criteria — not a hedge that lets us avoid choosing. The B2C ceiling is real and the competitive dynamics are unfavorable. B2B is the right long-term direction. The question is how to get there without betting the company on an unvalidated demand signal.

#### Reasoning
The B2C market position is deteriorating structurally, not cyclically. Two well-funded competitors means the cost of competing on acquisition will compound. 11 weeks of flat growth at 3,200 MAU is not a growth plateau waiting to recover — it is a signal that the current go-to-market is tapped out.

The B2B signal is genuinely interesting: 3 inbound inquiries with no marketing effort is a weak but non-zero demand signal. More importantly, the founder's enterprise background is an underutilized strategic asset. That combination — relevant skill, inbound signal, product that maps to team use cases — is more than most pivots start with.

Option B (full pivot) is high risk not because B2B is wrong, but because it requires 60–90 days to get the first signal, during which the team has abandoned what revenue it had and is operating on pure conviction. With 7 months of runway and 4 people, that is structurally dangerous.

Option A (stay B2C) is a slow death. The math does not work unless we identify a high-retention niche that could generate $40k+ MRR with 4 people, and there is no evidence that niche exists in the current data.

Option C, executed with discipline, buys real information at low additional cost. The critical design question for Option C is: what are the go/no-go criteria at day 60? If we cannot define those now, Option C becomes an indefinite hybrid and loses its strategic value.

#### Risks
- Option C risks being a hedge that extends ambiguity rather than resolving it. The team may experience it as "we're doing both half-heartedly" rather than "we're running a focused B2B test."
- If the 60-day B2B test produces weak signals, we will have used 2 months of the 7-month runway and still face the original decision with even less time.
- Strategically, being a hybrid product in the market sends unclear positioning signals that could hurt both the B2C retention and the B2B first impressions.

#### Unknowns
- What does "moderate rework" mean for the B2B adaptation? This is the pivotal execution question.
- Is the founder willing and able to run an enterprise sales process simultaneously with product/operational responsibilities?
- What are the actual conversion rates on the 3 inbound B2B inquiries — were they qualified leads or casual curiosity?

### Agent: Operator

#### Position
Option C is the right call, but I want to reframe it: what you're really choosing is "run a 60-day B2B sales sprint with the existing product and existing team." That is a concrete, executable plan. What it is not is "build a B2B product." Those are very different things and I need the council to be clear about which one we are actually recommending.

#### Reasoning
The execution constraint that matters most here is that we have 2 engineers and 7 months of runway. That means we cannot build a new product. We can configure, adapt, and sell the existing one.

Here is what Option C looks like operationally in the first 30 days:
- Founder takes point on B2B outreach. Targets the 3 existing inbound leads and identifies 10–15 more in the same company profile. This is a sales activity, not a product activity.
- Engineers stay on B2C maintenance mode — no new features, only bug fixes and stability. This is 20% of their current workload.
- One engineer spends 2–3 days doing a rapid assessment of what it would actually take to make the product work for a team of 10. Not building it — scoping it.
- At day 30, we have: either 1–2 qualified B2B leads who have seen the product and are interested in paying, or we do not. That is a clear signal.

Option B (full pivot) fails operationally because "shut down consumer product development" and "rebuild the product" are two separate and significant decisions that each need resourcing. Doing both simultaneously with 2 engineers is not a plan — it is a sprint to burnout.

Option A fails operationally because there is no lever to pull. We have no growth budget, the team is not growing, and we have tried the current approach for 11 weeks with no movement.

#### Risks
- The biggest execution risk in Option C is founder bandwidth. Running an enterprise sales motion while managing a team of 4, running the consumer product, and preparing for a board sync is not sustainable past 30 days.
- The engineers who are already showing burnout will not be re-energized by "maintenance mode." This needs to be communicated carefully or we risk losing them before we even get B2B signal.

### Agent: Risk Analyst

#### Position
Option C is defensible, but it carries a risk that the other agents may underweight: the 60-day test burns runway without a guaranteed off-ramp. The more important decision is not which option to choose, but what the explicit kill criteria are for each path — and whether the team has the emotional capacity to honor those criteria when the time comes.

#### Reasoning
**Option A risk profile:**
- Probability of meaningful revenue improvement in 90 days: LOW (no growth levers identified, competitive pressure increasing)
- Downside if wrong: slow cash drain to zero, team attrition before the runway ends
- Severity: HIGH. This is not a recoverable failure — it is a slow one.

**Option B risk profile:**
- Execution risk: HIGH (requires product rebuild, sales process from scratch, and team context switch simultaneously)
- Market validation risk: HIGH (3 inbound leads is not demand validation)
- Runway risk: If B2B sales cycle is 45–90 days at this price point, first revenue may not appear before month 5, leaving a 2-month buffer to react
- Severity: HIGH. A full pivot that fails leaves you with no product, no customers, and no story.

**Option C risk profile:**
- Execution risk: MEDIUM (lower scope than Option B, but dual-track execution is genuinely hard with 4 people)
- Market validation risk: LOW-MEDIUM (60 days is enough time to get a real B2B signal if the outreach is focused)
- Hidden risk: If both tracks underperform, you have extended ambiguity by 60 days, not resolved it. This is the risk no one wants to name.
- The most dangerous version of Option C is one where, at day 60, the signals are mixed (one interested B2B prospect, B2C still flat) and the team cannot bring itself to make a hard call.

**My core risk assessment:** Option C is the lowest-risk path only if you define and commit to kill criteria before day 1. Without them, it is not Option C — it is indefinite ambiguity with a runway clock running.

#### Risks
- Team burnout is the unquantified risk that could make any option fail regardless of which one is chosen. This needs to be explicitly addressed before the board sync, not treated as a background condition.
- The board sync on Friday is itself a risk: going in with "we're testing Option C" without defined criteria may reduce board confidence and make future fundraising harder.

#### Unknowns
- What does the cap table look like, and do the board members have strong preferences that would override this analysis?
- What are the actual backgrounds and needs of the 3 B2B inbound inquirers? This data exists and should be retrieved before Friday.

### Agent: Challenger

#### Position
I want to challenge the framing of this entire decision. The council is treating this as "B2C vs. B2B" when the real question may be: "Do we have a business at all, and are we planning a pivot or planning an exit?"

This is not contrarianism. It is a question the board will ask on Friday, and the team should answer it before they walk in the room.

#### Reasoning
Here is what the data actually says:
- 3,200 MAU at 6% paid conversion = ~192 paying users at $8 ARPU = ~$1,536 MRR
- That is not a struggling B2C business. That is a product that people like but that has not found a growth model.
- The 3 B2B inbound leads came with no outreach. That is genuinely interesting. But "interesting" and "business" are not the same thing.

The question I want the council to answer before recommending Option C: **what is the realistic ceiling on B2B revenue with this product and this team in 6 months?**

If the answer is "$10k–20k MRR," that is not a business that survives on its own — it is a business that raises a bridge round or gets acquired. That is a valid outcome, but it changes the decision criteria significantly. You would run Option C not to build a sustainable B2B company, but to generate enough signal for a fundraise or acqui-hire.

If the answer is "$50k+ MRR in 6 months," what does the path to that number actually look like, and does this team have it? The founder's enterprise background matters here, but enterprise sales at this price point is a volume game — it typically requires more than one person.

I am not recommending against Option C. I am recommending that the team be honest with themselves about what they are optimizing for before they execute it, because the right version of Option C for "extend runway to raise" looks very different from the right version of Option C for "build a durable B2B business."

#### Risks
- Going into the board sync without a clear answer to the "are we building or bridging" question is more dangerous than going in with Option A or Option B and owning it.
- The hybrid approach can be a form of avoiding a hard conversation with the team and the board. If the council recommends Option C, someone should be responsible for naming this risk explicitly to the team.

#### Unknowns
- What does the board actually want? Some boards want survival; others want a clear narrative. These lead to different recommendations.
- What is the founder's personal read on the team's morale and capacity? This is the one piece of data that does not appear in the context file and that only a human can provide.
